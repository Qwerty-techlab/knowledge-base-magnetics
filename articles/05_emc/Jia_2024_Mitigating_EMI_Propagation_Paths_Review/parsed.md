ABSTRACT
The wide applications of wide-bandgap (WBG) devices in power electronics systems bring
benefits in higher switching speed, frequency, and power density, but also cause severe electromagnetic
interference (EMI) issues. While many EMI mitigation methods are available to reduce the noise generated from the source (i.e., switching WBG devices), it is equally important to analyze and control the
EMI propagation paths. In WBG systems, the parasitic and coupling effects become dominant due to the
high switching frequency and the compact system size, which creates uncontrolled propagation paths that
degrade the systems' EMI performance significantly. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the parasitic and
coupling effects and develop corresponding mitigation techniques. This review focuses on analyzing the EMI
propagation paths to provide a comprehensive guideline to identify possible parasitics and couplings in the
power module package level, EMI filter level, and the power electronics system level. The corresponding
mitigation techniques for common-mode and differential-mode conducted EMI as well as radiated EMI are
summarized, and the remaining challenges and future research topics are also discussed for all three levels
in the conclusion.
INDEX TERMS
Coupling effect, EMI, mitigation techniques, parasitics, propagation path, WBG packaging.
I. INTRODUCTION
Electromagnetic interference (EMI) is a type of noise
generated by power electronics systems that can interfere with
their own or other systems' functions and therefore requires
mitigation. A complete EMI loop comprises three main
elements: the EMI noise source, the EMI noise propagation
path, and the EMI noise victim. In wide-bandgap (WBG)
power electronics systems, the EMI issue is becoming an
increasingly important consideration, primarily caused by
the noise source side, including faster switching speed,
higher switching frequency, and more oscillation than the
Si counterparts [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. In response,
researchers have proposed various mitigation techniques
at the source side, such as active gate driver methods [8],
[9], [10], [11] or optimized pulse width modulation (PWM)
schemes [12], [13], [14], [15], which can alleviate high
commutation spikes and reduce EMI noise generated from
the source. Reference [6] provides a systematic review of
relevant techniques to suppress EMI noise at the source
side.
However, EMI cannot be completely mitigated at the
source, especially at high current and/or voltage change rates
in WBG converters, so the understanding and controlling of
EMI propagation paths are also worthy of effort [16], [17],
[18]. The EMI propagation paths represent the network of
impedance residing between the EMI noise source and the
noise victims. Those impedances include lumped components
(such as filters), ubiquitous parasitic elements, or mutual couplings among them, which can be resistive, capacitive, or
inductive.

Although the terms parasitic effects and coupling effects
are sometimes used interchangeably in the literature, in this
paper, we define parasitic effects as occurring only within one
individual component and coupling effects among at least two
components. The parasitic effects come from the construction
of the individual component, such as the equivalent parallel
capacitance (EPC) of an inductor, the equivalent series inductance (ESL) of a capacitor, or the parasitic capacitances of a
power switch module [19], [32].
The coupling effects in this paper mainly refer to the capacitive couplings between conductors with different noise
voltage potentials and the inductive couplings between noise
current loops [26]. The governing equations are I = V∗(jωC)
and V = I∗(jωM), respectively, where ω is the angular frequency, and C and M are coupling capacitance and mutual
inductance. Both C and M have dependencies of placement
and geometry, where closer distance will increase both of
them.
The parasitic and coupling effects for WBG systems are
more pronounced than conventional Si systems, mainly due
to higher noise levels, higher frequency, and smaller system
sizes [19], [20]. In terms of parasitic effects, higher frequency
decreases the impedance of the inductor EPC and module
parasitic capacitances, as well as increases the impedance of
a capacitor ESL, all making the noise easier to propagate and
harder to filter.
Noise coupling also becomes more severe at high frequency
and shorter distances, due to increased ω, C, and M [19].
Fig. 1 shows an example of an LCL EMI filter. The measured
filter transfer gain suffers from up to 60-dB degradation in
attenuation due to the coupling effects between the input and
output terminals of the filter [21]. Therefore, it is crucial
to identify and analyze the parasitic and coupling effects in
power electronic converters to effectively address the EMI
propagation paths.
The modeling, analysis, and design for the parasitic and
coupling effects are challenging and require both holistic design approaches and mitigation techniques [18], [41], [42].
This is because the parasitics and couplings are present across
multiple levels of the power electronics from individual components to overall system packaging, and they span across
the entire product developing lifecycle. Depending on the
functional level and design stage, the parasitic and coupling
effects can be analyzed and resolved with different solutions.
For the parasitic and coupling effects that would worsen the
EMI performance, such as the EPC of the filter inductor that
would negate the inductance at high frequency, appropriate
cancellation techniques should be developed to eliminate its
influence [63], [64], [65], [66], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71],
[72]. On the other hand, the beneficial ones that can reduce
the EMI noise received by the victims, such as the parasitic
capacitances between the power lines and the ground that
would work the same as shunt capacitors for common-mode
(CM) noise reduction, should be strengthened [42]. In all, a
comprehensive EMI propagation path design guideline for the
WBG system concerning the parasitic and coupling effects is
urgently needed.
A few review papers have discussed EMI mitigation methods and parasitic and coupling effects but from different
perspectives, not focusing on systematic identification and
analysis of EMI propagation paths. In [6], WBG devices acting as EMI noise sources and their influence on EMI-related
topics were discussed. It gave a thorough explanation of how
the EMI noise was affected by the switching characteristics
of WBG devices and also gave comparisons of the EMI noise
caused by WBG devices and their Si counterparts. Mitigation
methods regarding the EMI noise source and propagation
paths were summarized and the EMI-related reliability issues
were addressed.
Reference [22] reviewed the state-of-the-art modeling and
simulation methods for conductive EMI in power converters.
Reference [23] summarized conductive CM EMI mitigation
methods for inverter-fed motor drives from both noise sources
and propagation paths. Comprehensive comparison tables of
different PWM methods, improved gate driver techniques,
complex topologies, passive EMI filters, and active filters
were provided in a methodical manner. The summary in [24]
had a similar structure for a switch-mode power converter.
Reference [25] focused specifically on active EMI filters
and provided a good overview of their characteristics and
applications. Reference [26] introduced the generation and
propagation mechanism of radiated EMI and summarized the
latest research on radiated EMI modeling methods and mitigation techniques, presenting a good review and instruction
related to radiated EMI.
This paper provides a comprehensive review and design
guidelines for power electronics systems regarding EMI propagation paths by focusing on the most critical and challenging
parasitic and coupling effects. It investigates the possible parasitics and couplings from WBG-based power module package
level, EMI filter level, and the whole system level and summarizes corresponding EMI mitigation techniques for the three
levels as Fig. 2 shows, including not only the CM conducted
EMI noise but also differential-mode (DM) conducted EMI

B. FILTER-LEVEL PARASITICS AND COUPLINGS AFFECTING
EMI
EMI noise can be separated into CM noise and DM noise
according to their different generation mechanisms. CM noise
flows between the power electronic system and the ground
through parasitic capacitances while DM noise propagates
between the power lines [30]. Thus, to suppress specific types
of noise, EMI filters can be divided into common mode filters
(CMF) and differential mode filters (DMF). Under high frequency, because the performance of the EMI filter is strongly
affected by the parasitics and couplings, it is very important
to identify all of these for both CMF and DMF.
Self-parasitics, such as inductor EPC caused by capacitances among windings, turns and cores, inductor equivalent
parallel resistance (EPR) caused by coil resistance, and equivalent series inductance (ESL) and equivalent series resistance
(ESR) of capacitors caused by capacitor leads and electrode
plates shown in Fig. 5, exist in both CMF and DMF. However,
the coupling effects are different in CMF and DMF. In CMF,
the capacitances of CM capacitors are usually limited by EMI
standards to prevent large leakage current due to safety concerns, so a large CM inductance is preferred; while because
there is no DM capacitance limitation for DM capacitors, a
small DM inductor is enough for the DM noise attenuation.
In this case, CM inductors with magnetic cores are adopted in
the EMI filter design, of which the leakage inductance works
as the air-core DM inductors to prevent core saturation. So,
the mutual inductive couplings among the input trace, output
trace, DM inductors, and DM capacitors are more likely to
happen in DMF due to the stray magnetic flux. CMF is affected less by the coupling effects due to the high permeability
core and more by inductor self-parasitics, except the input
and output loop coupling could have some impacts on its
performance [30], [31].

C. SYSTEM-LEVEL PARASITICS AND COUPLINGS
AFFECTING EMI
In power electronics systems, parasitics exist in transistors,
packages, gate drive circuits, probes and sensors, passive
components, and conductors such as bus bars and cables.
Pursuing high power density with compact size as one of
the goals of power electronics system design would induce
stronger couplings among components, conductors, and nodes
with high pulsating voltages [41], which would not only cause
a worse EMI performance and lead to bulkier EMI filters that
are against the goal of high power density but also cause more
semiconductor losses and thermal stresses, deteriorating the
system's reliability. Thus, the key parasitics and couplings in
power electronics systems need to be comprehensively analyzed.
In [33], the parasitic capacitances and capacitive couplings
of a 10-kV SiC-based medium voltage power electronics
converter system as an example of DC-AC systems are thoroughly identified and analyzed. In the gate drive circuit, the
parasitic capacitances caused by the DC-DC power isolation
stage such as the semiconductor-based isolation barrier and
the monolithic transformer provide extra CM paths which may
cause gate signal interference and worsen the WBG device's
switching behavior, affecting the power electronics system's
EMI performance from the noise source [34]. Including the
coaxial shunt resistor in the circuit for current measurement
increases the length of the power loop with more parasitic
inductance, while the parasitic capacitances induced by other
sensing techniques and voltage probes affect the precision of
the measurement as well as introduce extra CM paths and
generate more CM current as the probes are connected to
the grounded oscilloscope [35], [36]. In WBG systems, the
short cables act as transmission lines due to the high switching
speed, which results in reflected wave phenomenon (RWP)
and causes overvoltage on a connected motor, stressing the
motor winding insulation and aggravating EMI issues [37],
[38], [39]. RWP and the capacitive parasitics and couplings
related to the shielded cables, the motor, and the ground (or
grounded cabinet) that generate CM current and raise more
CM EMI noise are shown in Fig. 9 [40].
In AC-DC systems, capacitive couplings of an active-clamp
flyback (ACF) converter as an example are analyzed [41],
[42]. Reference [41] compares ACF with an AC-line EMI

IV. FILTER-LEVEL PARASITICS AND COUPLINGS

EMI noise mitigation. To mitigate the impact of parasitics
and couplings on the EMI filter performance, different filterlevel reduction and cancellation techniques are developed and
are presented in this section. A summary of these filter-level

A. EPC REDUCTION AND CANCELLATION TECHNIQUES
Since EPC is caused by close capacitive couplings of inductor
windings, turns and cores, increasing the distances between
turn to turn and turn to core is one way to reduce EPC [62].
However, this would enlarge the EMI filter size, leading
to a lower power density. Inductor structure optimization is
another way to reduce parasitic capacitances. Improved winding strategies in [63], [64] are shown for both conventional
toroidal inductors and planar inductors. In [63], the optimized
winding strategy of the conventional bifilar inductor shown in
Fig. 19(b) reduces the EPC from 123 pF to 22 pF since the
optimized winding strategy reduces the overlap between the
input and output ports, further decreasing stray capacitance.
In [64], the shape of CM inductor windings is changed
from round to rectangular (Fig. 20). The parasitic capacitances
between windings are in series with each other so EPC is
decreased.

V. SYSTEM-LEVEL PARASITICS AND COUPLINGS
MITIGATION TECHNIQUES
At the system level, the inductive couplings could be reduced
by minimizing the coupling loop area or placing the components in different relative positions. For example, in [44] two
different placements of the CM choke and the boost inductor
are compared regarding the EMI performance of the system.
The perpendicular placement for the CM choke and the boost
inductor reduces the inductive coupling between these two
magnetic components and gives a lower EMI noise than the
parallel placement.
To reduce the parasitic capacitance caused by voltage isolation and improve the CM immunity of the gate circuit, the
optimized design of the isolated transformer with shielding
reduces the capacitance to as low as 1.2 pF [34]. In addition, there are also other methods explored to solve this
problem. Transferring the power over fiber by laser light is
one effective method to avoid the usage of the isolated transformer and eliminate the parasitic capacitance, but the allowed
transferring power and the conversion efficiency are limited
[84]. Wireless power transferring technique is developed and
adopted in the gate drive circuit for its non-core design, which
effectively lessens the parasitic capacitance between the transmitter side and the receiver side [85]. Moreover, a SiC power
module with Wi-Fi transmitted PWM signal and integrated
wireless-power-transfer-based gate driver is proposed in [86],
which has the potential to completely isolate the high-voltage
power module from the low voltages.
The coaxial current shunt which increases power loop parasitic inductance can be replaced by other advanced current
measurement methods such as PCB-integrated Rogowski coil
with DC sensing ability [87], [88]. The CM current induced
by the voltage probes measuring voltage nodes with high
dv/dt can be minimized by selecting a probe with an input
capacitance of less than 1 pF [89]. To mitigate RWP in DC-AC
motor drive systems, different dv/dt filters are involved and
compared in [40]. Cable shielding protects the signal and data
carried by the cable from being affected by EMI noise and
crosstalk [90], but it also introduces more capacitive couplings
between conductors and induces more CM current in the
systems. It is suggested that the shielded cables may not be
applied to the pulsating voltage nodes with high dv/dt [33].
For the capacitive couplings, increasing the distance between parts is one simple method to effectively reduce the
couplings, but this is against the power electronics system
design target of high power density. There is some research
that theoretically analyzes the capacitive couplings in the
system and develops advanced capacitive coupling reduction
techniques without sacrificing the system's compactness. In
[41], [42], the capacitive couplings in ACF systems are comprehensively identified and CM equivalent circuits are built
for theoretical analysis. Reference [41] adopts ACF with a
DC-bus EMI filter for its higher power density and efficiency,

VI. CONCLUSION
This review focuses on EMI mitigation techniques at the EMI
propagation paths. It provides a comprehensive summary of
identifying parasitics and couplings in the EMI propagation
paths at three different levels of the power electronics systems: WBG-based power module package level, EMI filter
level, and system level. Corresponding mitigation methods
are also provided in this review. Based on the above analysis, the conclusions and future research perspectives for
mitigation techniques at each level are summarized below.
In addition, the parasitic and coupling mitigation techniques
are summarized and compared in Tables 1-3 in terms of their
manufacturing complexity.
1) EMI mitigation techniques at the package level have
become a popular topic with the development of WBG
power module packaging. Employing advanced substrate techniques is more effective in reducing substratecaused parasitic capacitances, although it may increase
the manufacturing complexity and cost. Concerning
EMI filter integration techniques, while the benefit of
the in-package filter has been verified in certain research, their generalizability still needs to be explored.
If the integrated filter's advantages are not universally
applicable to all applications of the module, and external filters are still needed, the overall benefit would be
compromised. System-level evaluation of the module
with an integrated EMI filter should be investigated.
Additionally, there is a need for further research on
coupling effects within power module packages, given
that current studies primarily focus on structural aspects
and electrical performance.
2) Filter-level reduction and cancellation techniques for
parasitics and couplings have been developed for a
comparably longer time and there have been multiple
methods. It is crucial to note that while the cancellation methods cannot completely eliminate frequencyand temperature-dependent parasitics and couplings,
they are helpful in reducing the effects. Techniques
requiring complex calculation, precise component design, or adding cancellation circuits that might induce additional couplings, raise challenges for practical
